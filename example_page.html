The return of Londonophobia

Reflections on an ancient prejudice
  • Janan Ganesh

Does another city on Earth wind people up so much? Provoke such hot-tempered comment from such diverse quarters? Make left as well as right, American as well as Russian, huff and puff like scandalised voyeurs peering over the garden fence? 

In rightwing circles, the slander against London is that it is anarchic and so multicultural (omnicultural?) as to no longer be part of the west. The left, meanwhile, sees the place as a capitalist free-for-all: not just unequal but corrupt. Both sides tend to regard these sins as quite new. That is, London was a righteous place before a recent moral fall, or even Fall.

And here is where an informed local must step in. We’ve been hated for much longer than that.

Londonophobia is an old prejudice. The essence of it, which is a belief that London is in various senses too lax, never changes. The Russian propagandists who smear the city today follow in a grand tradition. Their Tsarist forebears had an issue with Victorian London for harbouring Europe’s political dissidents. When Joseph Conrad brought that underworld to life in The Secret Agent, he won’t even have known that Lenin was in town, incubating the revolution.

Even back then, London had been an accursed thing for centuries already. There is an ancient tension between Paris (refined, hierarchical) and London (commercial, freewheeling). The philosopher Jürgen Habermas, who died last month, contrasted the 17th-century salons, where the discussion of ideas was brilliant but managed, with the argumentative chaos of the London coffee house. 

That intellectual difference recurs over the ages. In the 1990s, French officials worried that “Londonistan” was soft on Islamic radicals. This echoed an old view of the place abroad, and in fact in much of England, as suicidally tolerant. By all means, deplore this characteristic of ours. Just don’t pretend that it sprang up within JD Vance’s lifetime.

It is the best proof I know of the horseshoe theory of politics. Left and right end up hating the same thing

The left goes in for this kind of recency bias, too. Consider the adorable notion that London “became” commercial a few decades ago. In the archetypal state-of-London novel nowadays, a trillionaire called Dmitri will torment an angelic servant on a zero-hours contract in his Holland Park mansion, from which he is somehow also always absent. This paint-by-numbers account of the city is forgivable when it comes from US correspondents. There is no need for others to give it shrift. Or to romanticise the past. If anything, the supposedly egalitarian London of the 1970s (when, by the way, the moral panic was that Arabs were buying up the place) is the exception in the city’s much deeper history of almost literally piratical capitalism. It was being called the Great Wen some 200 years ago.

Notice the through line here. Whether a London-knocker is left-leaning, or the kind of conservative who can afford to live anywhere and chooses a golf resort, their argument will tend to be the same. Regulate, regiment, bring into line. The anti-foreign impulse and the anti-commercial impulse are so often related.

Londonophobia is the best proof I know of the horseshoe theory of politics. Left and right end up hating the same thing, liberalism, because both movements have at their core a desire to control. 

To those who think systematically, the city must be offensive. The place is not just loose when it comes to human beings, but to the built environment too. There is no rational street layout — much less a grid, thank God — and no consistent architectural style either. Places around the corner from each other can seem to inhabit different centuries. (I propose Holloway Road into Highbury Fields as the sharpest tonal contrast in the city, but will entertain other bids.) 

Francis Ford Coppola said that Apocalypse Now was so chaotic, expensive and harrowing to make that it ceased to be about Vietnam. The film was Vietnam. It embodied the madness of the war. Following that logic, London is not a liberal city. It is liberalism. The place itself — with its non-linear streets, its architectural farrago, its chalk-and-cheese neighbourhoods — is what happens when lots of individual choices accrue over centuries. It could not have come from a top-down designer with a single totalising idea. You can see how the place might rankle with the schematically minded. The result is, and always has been, a city with all the right enemies.
